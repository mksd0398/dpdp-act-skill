Do I need consent to use photos of customers and staff in my ads under the DPDP Act?

Answered 2026-11-21 · where it was asked

Yes, in almost every case. A photo in which a person can be recognised is personal data under s.2(t), and putting it in an ad is processing, because s.2(x) includes "dissemination or otherwise making available". The Act has no legitimate-interests ground, and none of the s.7 legitimate uses covers marketing, so the basis left is consent under s.6(1). None of this is enforceable before 14 May 2027.

Why "they sent us the photo" is not enough

s.4(1) allows only two bases: consent, or a use on the closed s.7 list. Two clauses look tempting and both fall short.

s.7(a) covers data a person "voluntarily provided" for a "specified purpose". A client who sends photos of her flat so you can quote for a kitchen gave them for the quote. Using them in an Instagram ad is a new purpose, and s.7(a) does not stretch to it.

s.7(i) covers processing "for the purposes of employment". An ID photo for the staff badge fits. Your site supervisor's face in a paid ad is marketing, not employment. That is a reading, not a holding: there is no Board decision on it yet.

Staff: ask, and make "no" safe

s.6(1) wants consent that is "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action". A clause in the appointment letter letting the company use your image anywhere is weak on "free" and fails "specific". Ask separately, per campaign or channel, and make sure saying no costs the employee nothing.

Customers: what valid consent looks like

  • Notice first. s.5(1) requires a notice before or with the request, naming the data (which photos), the purpose (which ads, which channels), how to withdraw, and how to complain to the Board. Rule 3 adds that the notice must stand on its own and give an itemised description and a withdrawal link. Rule 3 comes from secondary sources here, so verify it against the notified Gazette text before relying on it.
  • Language. The person must be offered the notice and request in English or any Eighth Schedule language (s.5(3), s.6(3)).
  • A clear yes. A signed release, a ticked box that was unticked by default, or a recorded WhatsApp reply to a specific question all work. Silence does not.
  • Keep the proof. If a dispute reaches the Board, s.6(10) puts the burden on you to prove notice and consent. Keep the consent record with the exact photos, channels and date.

Withdrawal: what happens to the live ad

Under s.6(4) a customer can withdraw at any time, as easily as she agreed. Ads that ran before that stay lawful (s.6(5)). After it, s.6(6) requires you, within a reasonable time, to stop processing and cause your Data Processors to stop. Pull the ad, remove the photo from your site and feeds, and have the agency that edits your creatives delete its copies under s.8(7)(b).

Children in the frame

Anyone under 18 is a child (s.2(f)). Before you use a child's photo, s.9(1) requires verifiable consent of a parent, and Rule 10 sets how you check that the parent is an identifiable adult. Verify Rule 10 against the Gazette text. A family photo in a living room therefore needs the parent's verifiable consent for each child shown, not just the adult's own yes. s.9(2) separately bars processing "likely to cause any detrimental effect on the well-being of a child", and consent does not cure that.

Things that do not change the answer

  • "Faces are biometric data, so stricter rules apply." The DPDP Act has no sensitive-data tier. A face photo gets the same treatment as a phone number. Clinics should check their own professional or sectoral advertising rules, which DPDP does not displace.
  • "She posted it publicly first." s.3(c)(ii) takes data a person made public herself outside the Act. The Illustration is a blogger's own post. Whether that lets a business lift a customer's post into a paid ad is untested. Ask her anyway: it costs one message.
  • An empty room. A photo with no identifiable individual is not personal data. A nameplate, a family portrait on the wall or a visible flat number can change that.

A worked example

An interiors studio in Pune finishes a 3BHK. It wants to run a Meta ad with three photos: the empty living room, the owner couple on their new sofa with their 8-year-old, and the studio's site supervisor on the job.

  • The empty living room: blur the framed family photos and it needs no consent.
  • The couple: one notice naming the photos, "Instagram and Facebook ads and our website", a withdrawal link and the Board complaint route. Each adult says yes in writing.
  • The child: the parent's verifiable consent under s.9(1), or crop the child out.
  • The supervisor: a separate request he can refuse freely.

If the couple withdraws later, the studio pauses the ad, removes the photos and asks its agency to delete its edits.

The penalty ceilings are Rs 50 crore for a consent failure (Schedule item 7) and Rs 200 crore for children (item 3). They are maximums ("may extend to"), imposed only if the Board finds the breach significant under s.33(1). Meta's own ad policies apply separately.
