---
question: Can companies run background checks on job candidates without consent under the DPDP Act?
date: 2026-11-22

Possibly, but do not build on it. s.7(i) lets an employer process personal data without consent "for the purposes of employment", and hiring plausibly counts, but that reading is untested and checks that pull data from past employers, courts and databases sit at its edge. A candidate's own resume is safer ground under s.7(a). For the full background verification (BGV), take specific consent under s.6(1) with a notice under s.5(1). The Rules' duties are not enforceable before **14 May 2027**.

## Two lawful bases, and no third

s.4(1) allows processing only with consent or for a use in the closed s.7 list. There is no legitimate-interests test, so "we need it to hire safely" is not a basis on its own. You must point to a clause.

Two clauses are in play for candidates:

- **s.7(a)** covers data the candidate has "voluntarily provided" for a specified purpose, until she says she does not consent. A resume sent for a job opening fits: you may read it, shortlist and interview. It does not cover data you collect about her from other people.
- **s.7(i)** covers processing "for the purposes of employment". A candidate is not yet an employee, and the word "employee" in s.7(i) attaches only to the service-or-benefit example, so recruitment can be read in. No Board decision says so, so treat it as a reading.

A background check is the hard case. You call her last two employers, search court records and verify her degree with a university. None of that is data she volunteered, so s.7(a) does not reach it. s.7(i) might, but if it fails and you have no consent, you have processed without any basis.

## Why consent is the safer route

Consent under s.6(1) must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action". For a BGV that means a separate consent, not a line buried in the application form, naming what you will check and with whom. A notice under s.5(1) must come with or before it, saying what data, for what purpose, how to withdraw (s.6(4)) and how to complain to the Board. If a dispute ever reaches the Board, s.6(10) puts the burden of proving that notice and consent on you, so keep the signed or logged record.

Making the offer conditional on a check is not a problem in itself. s.6(1) limits consent to data "necessary for such specified purpose", so ask only for what the role needs. A sales role does not need a credit report.

Consent has one more benefit for the candidate. The access and correction rights in s.11(1) and s.12(1) are worded for consent and s.7(a) processing. A candidate whose report wrongly says she was dismissed can then ask you to correct it under s.12(2).

## The vendor is your processor, and you stay liable

Most companies use a BGV agency. It processes on your behalf, so s.8(2) requires a valid contract with it, and s.8(1) keeps you responsible for what it does. A clause saying the vendor is solely liable does not move that duty.

Because the report drives a hire-or-reject decision, s.8(3) makes you ensure the data is complete, accurate and consistent. A name-match hit on a court database that turns out to be a different person is exactly the error this duty targets.

## Rejected candidates' data

s.8(7) says erase once it is reasonable to assume the purpose is no longer served, unless another law requires you to keep it, and make the vendor erase too. Some HR guides cite a fixed 180-day deadline for rejected candidates. The Act sets none, and the Third Schedule periods under Rule 8(1) cover large e-commerce, gaming and social media platforms, not employers. Rule 8(3) separately asks you to keep personal data and processing logs for at least one year, so plan around that floor. Verify Rules 8(1) and 8(3) against the notified Gazette text.

## Worked example: a 60-person fintech hiring an accounts executive

Applicants upload resumes through the careers page; screening and interviews run on s.7(a). After the final round, the chosen candidate gets a one-page BGV notice in English, with a language selector for the Eighth Schedule languages (s.5(3) requires that option). It lists four checks: identity, education, last two employers and court records. She gives consent by a separate tick-and-submit step, and the log keeps the notice version and timestamp. The agency contract covers encryption, access control, breach reporting to the company and deletion on instruction. The agency sends results only to HR. Other finalists' interview notes are deleted one year after the role closes.

## What it costs to get wrong

All figures are ceilings, not predictions: up to Rs 250 crore for failing s.8(5) security safeguards, up to Rs 200 crore for missing a s.8(6) breach notice to the Board and each affected candidate, and up to Rs 50 crore for anything else. The Board can impose them only if it finds the breach "significant" under s.33(1). Penalties go to the Consolidated Fund of India (s.34); the Act gives candidates no compensation claim.
